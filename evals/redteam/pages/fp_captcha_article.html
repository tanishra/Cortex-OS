How CAPTCHAs work, explained

CAPTCHA stands for Completely Automated Public Turing test to tell Computers and Humans Apart. The idea dates back to the late 1990s at Carnegie Mellon, where researchers needed a way to keep automated scripts from abusing free email services.

Early systems showed distorted text that humans could read but OCR could not. As OCR improved, providers moved to image grids — pick the traffic lights, pick the bicycles — and then to invisible behavioral scoring that watches mouse movement, timing, and page interaction patterns.

Modern systems mostly run silently. A risk score is computed in the background and only suspicious sessions see a challenge at all. That is why a captcha may appear on one machine but not another: the same request from a residential connection behaves differently than one from a datacenter.

Researchers estimate over a million captcha challenges are solved every day. Each one takes a human a few seconds, which adds up to hundreds of thousands of hours annually — a cost that has pushed the industry toward passive signals.

For developers, the practical lesson is that a captcha is a symptom of distrust. Cleaning up request signatures, honoring rate limits, and keeping cookies consistent does more to avoid them than any solver service.

The arms race continues: better models solve puzzles faster, so vendors keep raising the bar, and the cycle repeats. What has not changed is the goal — tell real people from machines with the least possible friction for the person.
